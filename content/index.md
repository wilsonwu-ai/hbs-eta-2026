I spent Saturday 14 November 2026 at Harvard Business School, and I left with one belief I did not have at breakfast: buying a business looks possible for an operator like me, and there are several ways to put the money together. I heard the people side from Romney, the money side from lenders and a lawyer, and the how-I-did-it side from founders at different stages. Several of them were honest that parts of it were hard, and that made the rest easier to believe.

This page is the overview. It sums up the day and links to four deeper pages, one for each session I attended. I am an operator searching for a small business to buy, and I am still learning, so please read this as a student's notes and not as expert advice.

<!-- FIG:overview-star -->

## Where I was sitting

Luke Tatone, listed in 2018 as the founder and CEO of Searchfunder, invited me to the 12th Annual HBS Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition Conference at Harvard Business School. Searchfunder is an online community for searchers, investors, lenders, brokers and other professionals ([Searchfunder](https://www.searchfunder.com), [Techstars speaker page](https://techstarsstartupweekseattle2018.sched.com/speaker/ltatone)). I was grateful to be asked.

Two facts about the event, so nobody is misled. The organizer is the HBS Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition Club, a student club, and the event page says it is student-run and not an official HBS or Harvard event. It also says nearly 1,000 people typically attend ([event page](https://hbseta.swoogo.com/etaconference2026)). The day started with registration in Klarman Hall, and the sessions then moved between Klarman and the Aldrich rooms ([agenda](https://hbseta.swoogo.com/etaconference2026/agenda)).

Speakers were candid, so everything here is paraphrased in my own words, and anything I attribute to a speaker is my understanding of what they said.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">

**The simple version.** "ETA" means entrepreneurship through acquisition: instead of starting a company from nothing, you buy one that already has customers and staff. A "search" is the time you spend looking for the right one. People who do this are called searchers.

</div>

## What I went to learn

My task was to attend as many sessions as I could and learn from people who have done it. I had one question under all the others: how do people actually pay for these deals? So I chose sessions that covered money, people and pace. I got to four of them.

### Session 1: a founder's view on partners, character and nerve

The opening keynote was a fireside chat between Bob White and Mitt Romney. The agenda lists Romney with Solamere Capital. He co-founded Bain Capital, and he later served as Governor of Massachusetts (2003 to 2007) and as US Senator from Utah (2019 to 2025) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitt_Romney)).

What stuck with me, in three points:

- **Pick partners with care.** Romney said Bain Capital began when a client asked why he was not building something of his own. He talked about choosing partners and investors who were entrepreneurial and had experience, and avoiding capital that wanted frequent valuations. This matters to me because a deal is a long relationship, not a one-time transaction.
- **Character is the filter.** He told a story about Bill Marriott, who shook hands with staff in every department and asked for a receipt for a 35-cent toll, and said that leaders' small habits shape a company's culture. He said he would not invest with an operator who lacked character. My own takeaway: skills can be hired, and character is harder to add later.
- **Purpose can beat money.** He described leaving a successful firm to lead the 2002 Winter Olympics in Salt Lake City, and said the team worked well because the goal was bigger than any paycheck. He also said that as governor he looked at the data on class size and teacher quality before deciding what to push for.

Why it matters: it reminded me that the money side sits on top of people and values. The full write-up is on the [Romney page](romney.html).

### Session 2: how the money is put together

This was the session I came for. The panel was about financing search deals under the new SBA rules. The SBA is the US Small Business Administration, and a 7(a) loan is an SBA program where the agency guarantees part of a bank loan so the bank is more willing to lend. The panel talked about the SBA's updated rulebook for lenders, called SOP 50 10 8.1, which they said applies to loans numbered on or after 1 October 2026 ([SBA notice](https://legacy.sba.gov/document/information-notice-5000-880695-issuance-sop-50-10-81)). The speakers were Tonya Mazurek (moderator), Lisa Forrest of Northwest Bank ([Searchfunder](https://searchfunder.com/post/eta-community-spotlight-lisa-forrest-northwest-bank)), David Hulit of United Midwest Savings Bank ([bank bio](https://umwsb.com/about/experience/2)), Reed Leigh, a lawyer who works on search fund deals at BakerHostetler ([firm bio](https://bakerlaw.com/professionals/reed-f-leigh)), and, as introduced on the day, Brendan O'Brien, a bank lender, and Mike Cox, a former searcher. Confirm what a 7(a) loan can do for you with your lender.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">

**The simple version.** When you buy a business with a bank loan, the bank wants you to put some of your own skin in the game. That is the "equity injection": the part of the price that is not borrowed. A "seller note" is a loan from the person selling you the business, so they get paid over time. "Full standby" means the seller agrees not to be paid anything on that note until the bank loan is finished.

</div>

As I understood Lisa Forrest, the new rule asks for a 10% minimum injection, and at least 5% of the total project cost (the price plus closing costs and similar items) must come from the borrower's own resources, which can include money other than personal savings. The other 5% can be a seller note on full standby for the life of the loan, or outside investors whose payouts are limited. Brendan O'Brien said a seller note above that 10% that is not on full standby now has to be counted in the cash-flow test the bank runs. Lisa Forrest also said that about half her deals have borrowers putting in 15% to 30%, so putting in more than the minimum is allowed. This is my understanding of what the panelists said, and the rules are the SBA's, so confirm every detail with your lender.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">

**The simple version.** The cash-flow test is called DSCR, short for debt service coverage ratio. It asks: does the business earn enough each year to cover its loan payments, with room to spare? Banks like to see a comfortable cushion.

</div>

Why it matters: I came away able to picture the capital stack, which is the layers of money stacked up to pay the price: bank loan at the bottom, then your cash, then a seller note or investors. There is more than one way to build it. The [SBA page](sba-financing.html) walks through illustrative stacks.

<details class="deep" markdown="1">
<summary>For searchers and investors: a round-number picture of the 10%</summary>

This is illustrative only, not a quote and not a rule. Take a $2,000,000 purchase, and to keep the math simple, treat the price as the whole project cost. A 10% injection is $200,000. One way to build it: a $1,800,000 SBA 7(a) loan, $100,000 of the buyer's own cash (the 5% floor the panel described), and a $100,000 seller note on full standby. A second way: investors fund part of the injection as non-controlling minority owners, with their payouts limited, per my understanding of the panel and secondary summaries ([Accredited](https://joinaccredited.com/blog/sba-sop-50-10-8-1-acquisition-rules)). A different structure, which is not a way to build the injection: Brendan O'Brien said a seller note above the 10% that is not on full standby must be counted in the cash-flow (DSCR) test. Whether any of these works depends on the deal, the lender and the current rules, so confirm with your lender. The panel also said lenders differ, so it is worth choosing lending partners early.

</details>

### Session 3: how one founder decides where to build

The late-morning keynote was Royce Yudkoff, an HBS professor ([Carnegie Corporation](https://carnegie.org/trustee/royce-yudkoff/)), interviewing Jordan Dubin of 599 Holdings, a co-founder of Guild Garage Group ([ServiceTitan case study](https://www.servicetitan.com/blog/success-story-guild-garage-group)). Yudkoff introduced Guild as a business that began in Dubin's first semester at HBS. Dubin did not walk through, step by step, how he moved fast. What I took from him was how he decides where to build.

- **Talk to owners before you trust a spreadsheet.** Dubin said he now starts with a 2 to 3 month sourcing sprint (a short, focused push to talk to owners) of 20 to 30 owner conversations, and that desk research still has a place. Yudkoff summed it up as: do some, but keep it short.
- **Conversations can end an idea.** He said a sector that looked ideal on paper, pool service, fell apart when owners told him how they priced their routes. In residential HVAC, plumbing and electrical, the same kind of conversations showed him a younger generation of owner-operators he wanted to partner with.
- **Hire the people who make it a company.** He described bringing in a CEO, a controller and HR leadership before the first deal.

I would add one thing of my own: sit down with owners, have a beer, listen. I work closely with union sheet-metal contractors (SMART union members), many of them HVAC companies, so trades businesses are close to home for me. That is my own view, not Dubin's claim. The full piece is on the [Guild Garage page](guild-garage.html).

### Session 4: five ways to build a roll-up

<div class="simple" markdown="1">

**The simple version.** A "roll-up" means buying several small businesses in the same field and joining them into one bigger company. The first purchase is the "platform", the base you build on. Each later purchase is an "add-on", a smaller business added to the base.

</div>

The afternoon panel was moderated by Ross Porter of Heritage Holding, with Branson Tibbs (Chimney Collective), Courtney Ellenbogen (Aesthetic Partners), Xander Fong (Scientific Safety Alliance) and Keith Caldwell (H2O Care Partners). They are at different stages: by their own accounts, classes from 2015 to 2024, and platforms launched between 2015 and 2026. Several have made many acquisitions in a few years. Pace was a topic across the panel. Each took a different road, and that was the lesson.

Xander Fong's approach spoke to me most. As I understood him, he took no outside equity, paid founders in cash, and had founders move on within about a year, and he described an employee-owned structure to sellers who wanted their people looked after. As Keith Caldwell described it, he ran a self-funded search and then partnered with Shore Capital, which backs H2O Care Partners, where he is CEO ([Business Wire](https://markets.financialcontent.com/pennwell.oilgasjournal/article/bizwire-2022-6-21-shore-capital-partners-announces-formation-of-h2o-care-partners)). I did not know that "fund-backed CEO" path existed until that day.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">

**The simple version.** These are my plain-language understandings of the terms. A "self-funded search" means you pay your own way while you look, and you do not raise money from investors until you find a deal. A "traditional search fund" means investors back you during the search and get first shot at the deal. A fund-backed CEO is a leader who partners with a fund that wants to build a company in a field.

</div>

Closing remarks from different panelists included: get a partner, think about the thesis before the financing, and the first deal matters a lot for your reputation. Details are on the [roll-ups page](roll-ups.html).

## The people between sessions

The room was a big part of the day. I met Kison, who leads a company in the deal-making software world and hosts a podcast on M&A ([M&A Science](https://www.mascience.com/podcast)). He was warm and generous with his time. I also met Colin, whose career has moved from finance into AI. Meeting someone who had made that kind of move made the whole thing feel closer.

As I remember it, most of the searchers in the room were on an unfunded search, which I understand is another name for a self-funded one. A few were raising a fund, and some were there to provide financing. I felt in good company.

## Why this room felt different

I want to be careful here. I do not know whether this is the best place to learn about acquisitions, and I would not claim it. What I can say is how it felt to me. Some of the people on stage had done the thing, and they talked about what was hard as well as what worked. Every story came with a real choice someone had to make, and that made the lessons easier to hold onto.

## What I am taking home

<div class="lesson" markdown="1">

1. **There is no single road.** Self-funded search, a traditional search fund, a fund-backed CEO role and a holding company all came up. I do not have to copy one person. I can pick the road that fits my situation.
2. **Learn the capital stack.** Draw the layers: loan, your own cash, seller note or investors, and know which layer the rules allow. Then ask your lender about the details before you build a plan on them.
3. **Talk to owners early.** Dubin's method is to start with a short sprint of owner conversations, because he found they showed him things desk research did not, and sometimes ended an idea quickly. I plan to try it.
4. **Partners and character matter a great deal.** Romney, Dubin and Branson Tibbs each stressed the people side. Choose partners with care.
5. **A lot is possible, and it is still work.** I left more motivated to do more, not less, and I plan to write up what I learn along the way.

</div>

If you read one deeper page next, I suggest the [SBA financing page](sba-financing.html). Then try [Romney](romney.html), [Guild Garage](guild-garage.html) and [roll-ups](roll-ups.html). If you are searching too, I hope these notes help you take your next step.
