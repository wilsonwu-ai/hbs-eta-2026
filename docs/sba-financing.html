Session notes: financing a search under the new SBA rules

What I learned about financing a search under the new SBA rules, with an illustrative capital stack

As the panel described it, under the SBA's updated rulebook a buyer puts in 10% of the project, at least half of that from their own resources, and there are still several workable ways to fill the rest.

By Wilson Wu, founder of Dubbs Capital, an operator searching for a small business to buy. Written after attending the 12th Annual HBS Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition Conference, 14 November 2026.

The short answer I took home: under the SBA's new rulebook, you can still buy a business with mostly borrowed money, and there is still room to build a deal in several ways. As the panel described it, the buyer puts in 10% of the total cost. At least 5% (half of that 10%) has to come from the buyer's own resources, and the other 5% can come from a seller who agrees to wait, or from outside investors who accept limits on how they are paid. Where I could, I checked what I heard against public summaries. Some remarks are the panelists' own views and I could not check them, so please confirm every rule with your own lender.

Where I was sitting

Luke Tatone of Searchfunder invited me to the 12th Annual HBS Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition Conference at Harvard Business School on 14 November 2026, and the overview page covers the whole day. Entrepreneurship through acquisition means buying an existing small business instead of starting one. One common route is a search fund: a person, called a searcher, spends time looking for one company to buy and then runs it. My task was to attend as many sessions as I could and learn from people who have done it. I am writing for people like me: an operator searching for a small business to buy and wondering how to pay for it. The message under everything I heard is that it is possible, and here is how others did it.

This page covers one session, "Rewriting the Playbook: Financing Search Deals Under the New SBA Rules." I was in the room, and this is my understanding of it, in my own words. The moderator was Tonya Mazurek, a government-guaranteed lending specialist and co-host of the SBA Today podcast (bio page). The panel was:

  • Lisa Forrest of Northwest Bank (Searchfunder). In her introduction she described the bank's lending to search funds.
  • David Hulit, Director of Specialty Real Estate Lending at United Midwest Savings Bank (bank bio).
  • Brendan O'Brien, introduced as a senior SBA lender at Huntington.
  • Reed Leigh, an M&A and search fund lawyer at BakerHostetler (firm bio).
  • Mike Cox, introduced as a former self-funded searcher (one who did not raise a pool of investors for the search) who has bought, run and sold a business. He was the only searcher on the stage.

The rulebook behind it all is called SOP 50 10 8.1. SOP means "standard operating procedure," the manual that tells banks how to make SBA-backed loans. The SBA issued this version in August 2026 and it applies to applications that get an SBA loan number on or after 1 October 2026 (SBA notice).

The simple version. The SBA does not hand out the money. It promises a bank it will cover part of the loss if the buyer cannot repay. Because the bank is partly protected, it is more willing to lend. Forrest said defaults have gone up, and the new rules ask buyers to have a set amount of their own money at risk, with the aim of fewer loans going bad.

What I went to learn

I came with one question: how do the pieces of a deal fit together when you are buying a business with an SBA loan? The panel did not draw a full stack with dollar amounts, so the picture further down is my own drawing, built from the pieces that came up all session. I came away with four topics: the 10% rule, seller notes, investors, and what the bank will and will not count.

Topic 1: The 10% rule, and where the money may come from

Point 1: 10% down, 5% from you. Forrest said the SBA still wants at least 10% down on total project costs, and that the buyer must supply at least 5% of the project from their own resources. She listed cash, a gift, a rollover of retirement savings (a ROBS, short for Rollover for Business Startups), or a home equity line of credit (a HELOC, a loan against your house). Why it matters: this is one of the bigger changes for searchers, because the buyer's own money has a floor. One caution from my reading of secondary summaries (Starfield & Smith): they stress unborrowed cash, so ask a lender whether borrowed money such as a HELOC counts in their shop.

The simple version. Equity injection is the part of the price you pay with your own money and not a bank's. If a business costs $100, the bank may lend $90 and you cover $10. Under the new rules, at least $5 of that $10 has to be truly yours.

Point 2: ways to fill the other 5%. Forrest said the second 5% can come from a seller note on full standby, or from equity investors. A seller note is a loan from the person selling you the business, who agrees to be paid back over time. Standby means the seller gets no payments, neither principal nor interest, until the SBA loan is fully repaid. If investors fill that slice, their payouts are limited to tax distributions, which means only enough cash to cover the taxes they owe on the business's income. Why it matters: Forrest named two ways to fill that slice, and the summaries add lender standby debt (see the panel for searchers and investors further down). For a searcher with less cash, that slice is where a seller or investors can help.

Point 3: why the rule changed. Forrest's explanation was that defaults have gone up, that the SBA wants a stronger loan portfolio and room for higher lending limits, and that business purchases are a large and growing share of the 7(a) program. The 7(a) loan is the SBA's main loan for general business purposes, including buying a company. She estimated the share at about 30% of SBA lending. The one public figure I found is lower: the Coleman Report counted purchase loans as about a quarter of 7(a) dollars, 25.4%, in the first six months of the 2026 federal fiscal year (Coleman Report). Either way, it is a big slice. Why it matters: knowing the reason helps me guess which way the rules lean.

Point 4: veterans get a change of their own. The moderator said veteran buyers would need no injection at all. I checked afterward. The SBA's notice, effective 11 November 2026, says something narrower (SBA notice). So the 10% minimum stays, but for eligible veterans outside sources such as standby debt, a full-standby seller note and non-controlling minority equity can cover it. I could not confirm how much of their own cash a veteran must bring. Forrest added that the bank still looks hard at cash flow for veterans, because the down payment and the ability to repay are two separate tests. Why it matters: it is good news for veterans, and a reminder to read the notice and not rely on a remark from the room.

Beyond the injection, the law-firm summaries I read list other changes in 8.1 (Starfield & Smith, PilieroMazza). For first-time acquisitions, the minimum debt service coverage ratio (DSCR, how many times the business's cash flow covers its loan payments) goes from 1.15x to 1.25x, it must be met without projections (the owner's forecasts of future profit), and a quality-of-earnings report is required at $3M or more. A seller may now consult for the buyer for up to 24 months after closing, up from 12. These come from secondary summaries, not the manual itself, so confirm with your lender.

Topic 2: Seller notes

A seller note is a loan from the person selling you the business. Instead of paying the full price at closing, the seller agrees to be paid back over time. Three points mattered here.

Point 1: inside the 10%, the note must sit on full standby. O'Brien said a 5% seller note that counts toward your 10% has to stay on full standby for the life of the SBA loan. He called it a government rule. Why it matters: you cannot promise the seller steady payments on that slice.

Point 2: a bigger note that is not on full standby gets counted against your cash flow. O'Brien said a seller note above the 10% is allowed, and lenders often like it. But if it is not on full standby for the whole loan, the lender now has to include its payments when it tests whether the business can afford its debt. Before 1 October, he said, many lenders, though not all, would let a standby of 12 or 24 months skip that test. A later summary I read adds that a partly standby note is counted as if it were paid off over 10 years (Beancount.io). Why it matters: a bigger seller note is no longer free room.

The simple version. DSCR, the debt service coverage ratio, asks one question: does the business earn enough to pay all its loans? Say it earns $125 in a year and the yearly loan payments are $100. That is 1.25x, and it is the minimum the summaries say the SBA now wants for most purchases. The rule under 8.1 is that a seller note that is not on full standby counts as a payment in that math. This is an example only, so confirm the real test with your lender.

Point 3: a tool in the negotiation. O'Brien predicted this will push purchase prices down, and he framed it as good for buyers. A buyer can tell a seller the bank will not lend at that price. He also said lenders order their own outside valuation. If the value comes in under the price, the gap has to be filled by equity or by a full-standby note. He gave an example: a business valued at $6M with a $7M ask leaves a $1M gap. His advice to buyers was not to fill that gap with their own equity, since the business is not worth it. Why it matters: the rule gives a searcher a polite, neutral reason to say the price does not work.

Leigh added the legal side. A seller note can give the buyer a right to offset, meaning to hold back payments if the seller owes the buyer for a problem found after closing. Leigh said that offsetting against a seller note you are not yet paying does not always help, for example on a litigation claim, so escrows matter for real claims. An escrow, also called a holdback, is part of the price set aside to cover such problems. He also described a case where the lender raised a non-compete issue (a promise by the seller not to start a rival business) only after the letter of intent (the LOI, the short document where buyer and seller agree on the main terms). His lesson: bring bankers and lawyers in before the LOI, not after.

Cox said the SBA is a great tool but you have to be flexible, and the moderator added that it is one tool in a toolbox. In his own 2020 purchase, lenders were not available, so he used a different path. The roll-ups page shows five more ways operators have put the money together.

Topic 3: Investors, and putting in more than the minimum

Point 1: investors are allowed. Forrest said investors are still welcome. The summaries I read add conditions: the investor cannot control the company, must hold under 20%, and gets tax distributions only on the slice that counts toward the injection until the SBA loan is paid off (Accredited). Money above the required injection can be paid normal distributions, and Forrest said those payouts cannot be guaranteed and your operating agreement must keep you in charge. Why it matters: how you promise to pay an investor changes depending on which dollars they put in.

Point 2: more equity can be smart. Forrest said that at her bank roughly half of the deals have buyers putting in more than the minimum, often 15% to 30% down, because the numbers have to work on cash flow. She urged searchers to consider borrowing less and keeping a little less ownership. Why it matters: the highest loan is not always the best deal.

Point 3: the bank looks backward. Forrest said SBA lending is based on the business's earnings as they are (EBITDA, which is profit before interest, taxes and some other items), with no allowance for a dip in the first year or two. Some searcher-funded deals are built around that early dip. Her point was to leave a cushion. Why it matters: your plan to grow the business cannot be what pays the lender.

Point 4: skin in the game, and who can play. The moderator raised a fairness worry: do the rules favor people who already have wealth? O'Brien said more of your own money at risk is not bad. He said searchers may need to aim at a smaller deal than they hoped, and that each deal will be judged on its own. Why it matters: it is honest, and it points to starting smaller.

The simple version. More of your own money in a deal means the bank trusts you more and the payments are smaller. It also means you own a bigger piece. Bringing in investors above the minimum can lower the loan without making you borrow more.

Topic 4: What the bank will count, and who to pick

Point 1: skinny deals, as they are. A lender on the panel said banks analyze acquisitions as they stand today. Cost-cutting plans do not count. One panelist said projection-based lending for a first-time buyer is very hard to imagine, with a carve-out for special-purpose properties such as hotels and self-storage for experienced operators with strong guarantors (people who promise to repay the loan if the business cannot). Another said larger banks do not lend on projections.

Point 2: living costs. One banker on the panel said a full-time searcher with no other income must leave room in the cash flow for their own household costs. That lowers the debt a deal can carry.

Point 3: cash left after closing. O'Brien said his bank looks for liquid assets of about 10% of the loan after closing, for example $100,000 on a $1M loan, so the buyer is not spending every last dollar. The moderator added that every bank differs.

Point 4: pick lenders early. A banker on the panel said banks follow the same SBA eligibility rules but have different appetites, and they refer deals to each other. Hulit reminded us that something being allowed by the SBA does not mean a given bank will do it. Panelists said a lender saying no can be useful information, and one panelist said lender default rates vary from about 15% to under 1%, a figure I could not check, so a quick yes is not proof of a good deal.

Point 5: brokers and your own homework. Hulit said a good loan broker sends a clean package, so he can say yes or no in about two hours instead of two weeks. A broker pushing one lender hard may be getting an unusually high referral fee from that lender. Another panelist added that it is your loan, so stay close to the details.

Why it matters across all five: the SBA sets a floor, but the bank decides. Your job is to find the bank whose appetite fits your deal before you sign anything.

Putting the pieces together: an illustrative stack

A capital stack is the list of where the money for a purchase comes from, drawn as layers. Here are three illustrative pictures for a $2,000,000 purchase. For simplicity I treat the price as the total project cost. These are my drawings, not examples from the panel, and the numbers are round on purpose.

  • Stack A, the base case. SBA 7(a) loan $1,800,000 (90%). Buyer's own cash $100,000 (5%). Seller note on full standby $100,000 (5%).
  • Stack B, with investors. SBA 7(a) loan $1,800,000 (90%). Buyer's own cash $100,000 (5%). Investors $100,000 (5%), paid tax distributions only while the loan is outstanding.
  • Stack C, a larger seller note. SBA 7(a) loan $1,600,000 (80%). Seller note not on full standby, $200,000 (10%), whose payments count in the coverage test. Buyer's own cash $100,000 (5%). Seller note on full standby $100,000 (5%). A smaller loan means lower bank payments, but the seller's payments now count too.

You could also fill a bigger share with your own cash, as Forrest described, and borrow less. Illustrative only: your lender decides what is allowed, and every deal is different.

For searchers and investors: the details behind the 5%

These details come from law-firm and lender summaries, not the manual text, which I could not read. Check them against Appendix 15 of the SOP and with your lender.

  • Limited and unlimited sources. The summaries describe limited sources (standby debt, full-standby seller debt, non-controlling minority equity) that together can supply no more than half of the injection. The rest must come from unlimited sources, mainly cash that is not borrowed. The 5% own-resources figure is derived from that half cap. Lender standby debt can also fill the limited half, not just a seller note or investors (Starfield & Smith).
  • Investor tests. Non-controlling and under 20%, with tax distributions only on injection capital until payoff. A preferred return requiring current cash on injection capital looks problematic in one firm's reading.
  • Seller debt and DSCR. Full-standby seller debt stays out of the coverage calculation. Anything else is in it. The coverage floor rises to 1.25x for initial acquisitions, owner buyouts and ESOP deals (employee ownership plans) and stays at 1.15x for business expansions.
  • Quality of earnings (QoE). A lender-ordered QoE, an outside review of the seller's profit figures, is required when the purchase price, excluding owner-occupied real estate, is $3M or more. A panelist on the day put the line at deals over $3M, so the exact edge is worth checking with your lender. Owner buyouts are exempt.
  • A remark I am not treating as a rule. I heard a comment that working in a business for 24 months could lower the injection below 5%. The summaries I read say the 10% for an initial acquisition cannot be reduced, and that a reduction is available for business expansions and owner buyouts based on documented liquidity. I would not rely on that remark.
  • Seasoning. Seller-note seasoning before a refinance reportedly moved from 24 to 36 months (Accredited).
  • Partner searches. One panelist said the 5% is counted across all guarantors, so a partner search may let you share it. This was one panelist's reading, early in the rules, and I could not confirm it. Lenders may adapt, so ask your lender.

What I am taking home

  1. Count your own 5% first. The floor on your own resources is the number that decides how much room you have. Ask each lender what counts.
  2. Read a seller note as two kinds. A note on full standby fills part of the injection. A note that is not on standby costs you cash flow.
  3. Put more in if it makes the deal safer. Borrowing less and bringing investors above the minimum was presented as a strength.
  4. Line up the bank and the lawyer before the LOI. Leigh's non-compete story showed how a late surprise can slow a deal down.
  5. Treat a no as information. A lender's no can teach you something, and every rule is worth checking against the SBA's own notice, not just what I heard in the room.

I walked out feeling encouraged. Nobody on that stage said it was easy, but they described several workable paths, and the lenders were open about how they think. That is what I came hoping to find. If you are working out your own capital stack, I hope this gives you a starting sketch to take to your lender.
