Session notes: Roll-Ups

Five operators, five ways to build a roll-up

Five operators described five different ways to build a roll-up. They differ on where the money comes from, what happens to the founder, and how fast to buy, and one panelist said to pick the industry thesis first and the financing second.

By Wilson Wu, founder of Dubbs Capital, an operator searching for a small business to buy. Written after attending the 12th Annual HBS Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition Conference, 14 November 2026.

Five operators sat on one stage and described five different ways to build a company by buying many smaller ones, and that was the lesson: there is no single recipe. They differ on where the money comes from, what happens to the founder after the sale, and how fast to buy, and one panelist said to pick the industry thesis first and the financing second. My own takeaway is encouraging: a searcher gets to design the path instead of copying one.

The simple version. A roll-up is a company that grows by buying lots of similar small businesses and running them together. The first purchase is the platform: the base the others attach to. Each later purchase is an add-on. The idea is that the bigger group can do things the small owners could not, like sharing accounting, buying software or recruiting for everyone at once.

Where I was sitting

Luke Tatone of Searchfunder invited me to the 12th Annual HBS Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition Conference at Harvard Business School, and I attended on 14 November 2026. The overview page tells the story of the whole day. My task was to attend as many sessions as I could and learn from people who have done it. I am an operator searching for a small business to buy, so I wrote this for people like me who are wondering how to pay for a deal and what comes after. The message under everything I heard: it is possible, and here is how others did it.

This was the session titled "Roll-Ups: What Does a Good Consolidation Look Like?" Ross Porter of Heritage Holding moderated and also took part, joined by Branson Tibbs of Chimney Collective, Courtney Ellenbogen of Aesthetic Partners, Xander Fong of Scientific Safety Alliance, and Keith Caldwell of H2O Care Partners. This comes from what I heard in the room and wrote down. Where I could not tell who said something, I say "one panelist". Every number about a company is the speaker's own, rounded, unless I link a public source.

What I went to learn

I wanted to know how people who started as searchers think about scale. I came away with four topics: the five models, the question of what to do with the founders, how to run the group after buying, and how to find deals and set the pace. Three of them gave class years, from 2015 to 2024, so the panel mixes long-running and very new operators, and all of them move fast.

Topic 1: Five operators, five models

There are five points here, one per operator. For each I look at the money, the founder's role after the deal, and the pace.

Ross Porter, Heritage Holding

Porter said he graduated in 2015 and that his path went from a self-funded search (a searcher pays for the hunt out of their own pocket and later brings in investors) through running several platforms, then independent-sponsor deals (a dealmaker raises money from investors one deal at a time), and now a traditional private equity fund. A Heritage podcast page confirms the 2015 class year, and Pulse 2.0 reported in September 2024 that Heritage's first fund closed at $220M of committed capital. On pace, Porter described about 70 businesses across 12 industries. Heritage's own site lists more than 50 acquisitions, so treat his 70 as his count. He also said Heritage has a rule against all-cash closings, because of how much transition help a handoff needs.

Why it matters: on this panel, Porter has gone furthest along the path from search to fund.

Courtney Ellenbogen, Aesthetic Partners

Ellenbogen described a self-funded search that began with a thematic focus on healthcare, which she noted is the approach people are often told to avoid. She said she graduated in 2019 and has done about 15 to 17 acquisitions in medical aesthetics, including med spas (clinics offering cosmetic treatments). On the money, a November 2023 press release announced a partnership with Norwest, and Beauty Matter described it as a minority investment (the investor owns less than half), with terms not disclosed. On founders, she needs them to stay, because they lead teams and still bring in the revenue.

Why it matters: it shows a roll-up where the sellers are the engine, so keeping them is the job.

Xander Fong, Scientific Safety Alliance

Fong's story gets the most space because his approach spoke to me most, and it was the clearest contrast with the others. He said he started a self-funded search in 2022, closed his first deal in 2023, and has since done about 30 acquisitions of businesses that test, calibrate and maintain equipment for labs and clean rooms. He is the only panelist with no outside equity investors. Benchmark International describes the company as entirely owned by its founders and employees. Fong said the group borrows from a private credit facility (a loan from a lender that is not a traditional bank), shares ownership with employees, and plans to hold for the long term rather than sell in a few years.

Four things stood out in how he operates.

  • Small capital forces discipline. His first deal needed no loan. On the second, the seller wanted to roll over some ownership, so he arranged a small private credit facility, and he also used seller financing (the seller lets the buyer pay part of the price over time) and had to be careful with every dollar. He credited that squeeze for making the team scrappy.
  • Founders leave on a schedule. He pays all cash, allows no rollover (where a seller keeps a stake in the company), and tries to have founders step out within about a year, with a transition of 6 to 9 months. His reasons: founders often do not own the customer relationships, and the team resists change while the old boss is around. A few exceptions stay on.
  • He buys what others skip. He offers sellers a business that stays owned by employees, and he buys places that bigger buyers pass over, including a very small operation in rural South Dakota.
  • One bad early deal can end the story. His lender told him a single bad business would pause lending, so quality came before speed.

Porter singled him out as the exception on the founder question.

Why it matters: a permanent, employee-owned holding company is a different bet from selling in 5 years, and it shapes who sells to you and why.

Branson Tibbs, Chimney Collective

Tibbs said he launched in May 2026 with a partner. A June 2026 announcement covers the first three founding businesses, and a later press release names him and Rhett Hogan as co-founders. Tibbs said he raised a traditional search fund (a small group of investors backs a searcher while they look, then backs the purchase), and added a credit facility called a DDTL, a delayed draw term loan: you borrow in pieces as you buy. By his account he had closed 7 businesses, with an eighth the following week and 7 more under letter of intent. Public announcements I found list fewer, so this is his count.

On founders he does the reverse of Fong: he keeps all the owners, offers equity and longer earnouts (part of the price paid later if targets are met), and protects his reputation, because what he tells one owner reaches the others.

Why it matters: committed capital lets you build the back office first, at the cost of a stretch where corporate hires cost money before the deals pay back.

Keith Caldwell, H2O Care Partners

Caldwell is CEO of H2O Care Partners, which buys water treatment companies. By his account he began with a self-funded search, then partnered with Shore Capital, and launched the company in April 2022. A Business Wire release from June 2022 confirms that Shore formed H2O Care Partners and names him CEO. He described about 45 acquisitions since launch. Public pages show fewer partner companies, and counts can differ by what is counted, so I treat it as his figure.

This is the fund-backed CEO path, and I did not know it existed. A firm with capital and a deal team sets up a platform, and an operator runs it as CEO. One panelist, who I believe was Caldwell, said he went from being set on doing it alone to partnering, and called that the best decision he has made.

Why it matters: it shows that starting alone and later bringing in a fund are not opposites.

For searchers and investors: how the five structures differ

The panel covered five of the main routes into acquiring a business:

  1. Self-funded search. The searcher funds the hunt and the first deal, then adds capital. Porter, Ellenbogen, Fong and Caldwell each began this way, by their own accounts.
  2. Traditional search fund. Investors fund the search, and then the purchase. Tibbs described this one.
  3. Fund-backed CEO. A fund creates the platform and an operator leads it. Caldwell, with Shore Capital.
  4. Independent sponsor. The dealmaker raises money deal by deal. Porter passed through this stage.
  5. Holding company. A permanent parent company owns the businesses and holds them. Fong's group works this way, with employees as owners. Heritage calls itself a holding company but also runs a private equity fund, so it is not a permanent-hold example.

The tradeoff I took from the day: the more outside capital, the more room to hire ahead of the deals, and the more you answer to an exit date. The less outside capital, the more you rely on seller financing, careful pacing and each deal being good. For how a single first deal can be financed with an SBA loan, see my SBA financing notes.

Terms to know. Earnout: part of the price paid later if the business hits a target. Phantom equity: a bonus linked to the company's value without real shares. Holdco: the parent company. Exit multiple: how many times its yearly profit a buyer will pay for the company. Always confirm structures with your own lawyer and lender.

Topic 2: What to do with the founders

Three points, and the panel split on them.

  1. Keep them or move them out. Fong pays cash and wants founders gone within about a year. Ellenbogen, Tibbs and Porter lean the other way, because the founders are the revenue, the relationships or the handoff.
  2. Tools for keeping people. Ellenbogen said she has tried holdco equity, option equity, earnouts and phantom equity, and moved incentives closer to the people on the ground. She was surprised how far out people will stretch a payout.
  3. Reputation travels. Tibbs stressed that owners in a small industry talk. How you treat the first seller is your sales pitch to the next.

The simple version. Imagine buying a neighborhood shop. Either the old owner stays to teach you, which keeps customers happy but slows change, or they leave quickly, which speeds change but risks losing the people who trusted them. Neither is wrong. It depends on whether the owner is the product or the process is.

Topic 3: Running it after the deal

Three points, and they connect to the founder question.

  1. Centralize only what you do better. Ellenbogen said a half-done centralization of marketing led local teams to disengage and was partly reversed. Another panelist said that centralizing marketing too early through an outside agency cost them leads. One panelist listed what their group moved up to the parent company: accounting and finance, field software, digital marketing, payroll and benefits, and a recruiting team.
  2. Buy a model operator. Tibbs hired a controller right after the first deal, built one chart of accounts, and moved the group to shared accounting and a shared field-service software system (ServiceTitan). About half the businesses already used ServiceTitan, but each set up differently, so he paid up for the best chimney business and used its setup as the template.
  3. The CEO's job changes. Fong said his role moved from finding deals to managing a team, with a co-founder running operations. Ellenbogen said her job shifted from making quick moves herself to coordinating a growing team, and she was surprised how little she does directly, so she repeats the company vision often because the layers below do not hear it the first time. One panelist, who I believe was Caldwell, spent the first year or so on deals, then on operations, and has since added regional presidents and a plan for a COO. Porter added that being responsive to every business gets harder as the count climbs from a few to dozens.

The simple version. To centralize is to have head office do a job once for every business, like payroll or bookkeeping. It saves money and effort. It goes wrong when head office takes over something the local owner did well, like winning customers in their own town.

Ellenbogen also said the next investor may value steady organic growth, meaning growth from the existing businesses, more than a perfectly centralized system. Why it matters: how deeply you integrate affects what the group is worth later.

Topic 4: Finding deals and setting the pace

Four points.

  1. Persistence beat polish. Tibbs spent weeks trying to disprove his chimney idea. Handwritten letters were ignored, so he picked the 200 most important businesses and called them again and again. His first deal came through a personal introduction. By his account, 9 of 10 deals are now found internally. Jordan Dubin made a similar case for talking to owners first, which I wrote up on the Guild Garage page.
  2. Start with the business everyone recommends. One panelist relayed a board member's advice: make your first purchase the firm everyone in the region would recommend, because respect in the industry is what starts the snowball.
  3. Check readiness before speeding up. One panelist suggested a test before stepping on the gas: books closed in 15 to 30 days, a real daily view of cash, and a clear picture of how debt changes things.
  4. Pace is a story for the next buyer. Tibbs said he could do 30 deals a year, but he protects the organic-growth story for the next sale. One panelist described buying about 8 a year for 3 years, about 18 this year, and staffing for 25 to 30 next year while debating slowing down with their board. Fong noted that businesses with steady, recurring customers can move faster than project-based ones.

The simple version. Buying fast feels like winning, but every purchase has to be absorbed. The panelists kept returning to one idea: buy at the speed you can run what you bought.

What I am taking home

  1. Pick the thesis before the financing. One panelist said to choose the industry thesis first and the financing second.
  2. There are more than two roads into this. Self-funded search, traditional search fund, fund-backed CEO, independent sponsor and holding company all showed up on one stage.
  3. Get a partner. Tibbs said he would not do something entrepreneurial again without one. In the opening keynote, Romney stressed choosing partners with care.
  4. The first company is your reputation. Tibbs and one other panelist made this point in different ways. Do a good first deal, and be careful with very small businesses, where losing one of four technicians can cost a large share of revenue.
  5. I am still learning, and that is fine. I am still comparing the five approaches, and everything here is what I heard from people who have done it, as I understood it. What I know for sure is that I left encouraged: there is a path for many kinds of searcher.
